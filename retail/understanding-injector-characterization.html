Understanding Injector Characterization

By Keith Fields · Tuned by Keith

Injector size is only part of the fuel model. The ECU also needs to know how that injector behaves as voltage, fuel pressure and pulse width change.

If idle fueling moves when the fans switch on, or a car drives well at one load and poorly at another, injector data is worth checking. Those symptoms are clues, not proof that the injectors are the fault.

Start with the right data

Use characterization for the exact injector part number, fuel and ECU format. A flow rating alone is not a complete calibration. Check the manufacturer's reference pressure, units and instructions before transferring values. A table from a similar-looking injector or another ECU can be wrong even when the numbers appear reasonable.

The ECU's driver strategy also matters. Peak-and-hold and saturated systems are not interchangeable settings. Confirm injector and driver compatibility rather than trying to correct an electrical mismatch in the fuel table.

Dead time is a model, not just an opening delay

ECUs use an offset or dead-time model to account for injector response. The definition can include opening and closing effects and depends on the ECU and supplied data. It usually varies with battery voltage and pressure differential.

At a short pulse width, an offset error represents a larger share of delivered fuel. A repeatable trim change with electrical load is useful evidence, but also check charging voltage, grounds and fuel pressure. Do not flatten the voltage table to hide the symptom.

Short pulses need their own attention

Near the injector's minimum controllable region, fuel delivery may no longer follow a simple straight line. Some ECUs use a short-pulse adder, some a different model. Use the format the ECU expects; do not paste an adder into an offset table.

A larger injector can spend more time in this region at idle. That does not automatically make it a poor choice. Good low-pulse characterization and an appropriate injector/engine combination matter more than the advertised flow number alone.

Check pressure across the injector

For port injection, the relevant pressure is fuel-rail pressure relative to intake-manifold pressure, not rail pressure by itself. A manifold-referenced regulator and a fixed-pressure system behave differently as boost changes. Direct-injection systems need their own high-pressure model and data.

Read the fuel-pressure control guide before blaming an airflow table for a pressure problem. A pump, regulator, sensor or supply fault needs diagnosis; changing injector scaling is not a repair.

Fix the foundation before chasing transients

Injector data, fuel pressure and the base airflow model interact. If those are wrong, closed-loop correction may hide the error in steady driving while a throttle change exposes it.

Use the closed-loop fuel guide to interpret trims, then read transient fueling for tip-in behavior. Do not use acceleration enrichment to cover a steady-state fueling error.

What to bring for a review

Send the injector part number and original data sheet, ECU model and software version, fuel type, regulator arrangement and current calibration. Include existing logs showing voltage, measured fuel pressure, manifold pressure, injector pulse width, lambda and fuel corrections where available. Note what changed and when the behavior occurs.

Do not create a loaded test just to fill in missing channels. Follow the agreed logging plan and resolve misfires, leaks or unstable fuel pressure first. The datalog guide explains why channel context matters.
