CHOOSE A SERVICE / BEFORE CHECKOUT

Base map, custom tune or retune?

Choose the work around your current setup and the result you need. A starting file, a completed custom calibration and a revision to an existing tune are different scopes.

  • Base map: a starting calibration for an approved combination and agreed use. It is not a finished custom tune. Confirm the intended use and follow-up plan before loading it.
  • Custom tune: calibration for your individual vehicle through the agreed logging and revision process. Send the installed parts, fuel and current ECU or tuning-device details before choosing the service.
  • Retune: ask me to review the existing calibration and proposed changes first. Describe the previous tune, who supplied it, and every hardware or fuel change; I confirm whether the retune service fits or a new scope is needed.
  • Single fuel or flex fuel: tell me the fuel you intend to use and the installed ethanol-sensing and fuel-system hardware. Flex-fuel support depends on the exact vehicle, ECU, software and hardware; selecting the service does not establish compatibility.
  • Standalone ECU or factory ECU: provide the ECU model, software and current configuration. Standalone commissioning and integration are scoped around the installed system; do not assume an Accessport service covers that work.

Device hardware, licenses, diagnostics, in-person work and travel may be separate costs. Check the current service menu, or ask which service fits without paying. I confirm the setup and any additional scope before proceeding.

How should I compare the build results?

Start with a car close to your own engine, turbo, ECU and fuel combination. Record the test date, fuel, installed parts and whether the number is measured at the wheels. Keep different stages of the same build separate; a later hardware change does not describe an earlier dyno run.

Ask what changed between the baseline and final run, whether they used the same dyno and test conditions, and how the car behaved beyond peak output. If a published example does not identify a condition, treat it as unknown. A documented result describes that individual car; it is not a promised output for yours.

Compare the Subaru examples or Porsche projects, then send the closest example with your own setup and intended use. That gives us a useful starting point for a realistic plan.

SUBARU E-TUNING / THE PROCESS

How does a Subaru e-tune work?

I start by reviewing your WRX or STI, installed parts, fuel, current calibration and goals. Choose a listed service from the service menu to check out, or ask a question without paying if you need help choosing. Complete vehicle details and any required logging equipment are needed before tuning begins. Complete the readiness checks before loading a file or collecting logs.

An initial calibration establishes the starting point. Follow the instructions supplied with that file, send the original logs and describe how the car behaves. I review the data, revise the calibration where needed and specify the next test. The driving experience, consistency and useful response matter alongside output.

What happens during the eight revisions?

  1. Revision 1 — establish a baseline: startup, warm idle, sensor readings and low-load operation.
  2. Revision 2 — everyday operation: part-throttle fueling and response, using your feedback and requested logs.
  3. Revisions 3–4 — controlled load: after approval, evaluate boost control, fuel delivery and knock response within the agreed test limits.
  4. Revisions 5–6 — refine delivery: transitions, transient throttle and the relationship between pedal input and boost.
  5. Revisions 7–8 — validate where needed: repeatability and outstanding refinement. A sound setup may finish earlier.

This is an illustrative progression, not permission to advance independently. I may combine, repeat or pause stages. Full-load testing requires a suitable dyno or controlled closed course, not public-road pulls.

What can require additional revisions?

Intake leaks, ignition faults, weak fuel delivery, inconsistent fuel, sensor or voltage problems and unusable logs can interrupt the process. Hardware or fuel changes can also change the scope. Repair the cause first; tuning cannot correct a mechanical defect. I discuss additional work and any revised quote before proceeding.

Most $400 custom e-tunes include eight revisions. The agreed quote defines whether the initial file counts toward that allowance. Initial files and agreed revisions are usually delivered the same business day and within one business day once the approved setup, required information and applicable payment are received. This is per-file turnaround, not a promise to finish the entire tune in one day. Business hours are Monday–Friday, 8 a.m.–6 p.m. Pacific.

WRX & STI / DATALOGGING

Which parameters should I log?

Ask me for the channel list for your exact model year, engine, ECU/ROM and software. These are common channel families, not a universal preset. Sensor availability, names, units and useful sample rates vary.

  • Operating context: elapsed time, RPM, speed and gear where available, accelerator and throttle position, engine load, intake and coolant temperature, battery voltage.
  • Airflow and boost: MAF airflow/voltage where used, manifold pressure or boost, requested boost or boost error, and wastegate duty cycle. Identify gauge versus absolute pressure.
  • Fueling and timing: measured AFR/lambda, commanded fueling where exposed, A/F Correction 1, A/F Learning 1, ignition timing, DAM, Feedback Knock and Fine Knock Learning.
  • Additional sensors: requested external wideband, fuel-pressure and ethanol-content channels when installed and supported.

EJ: 2002–2014 WRX and 2004–2021 STI

Confirm injector pulse width or duty cycle and external wideband logging as required. Fuel-pressure data requires a supported sensor and input; not every EJ ECU measures it. The stock oxygen-sensor reading alone is not the basis for validating high-load fueling.

FA20 DIT: 2015–2021 WRX

Confirm actual and target high-pressure fuel-rail readings and the requested direct-injection, temperature or cam-control channels. The logging strategy differs from the EJ.

FA24 DIT: 2022+ WRX

Use the FA24-specific list for your installed software, including actual/target fuel-rail pressure where exposed. Do not assume an EJ or FA20 preset transfers unchanged.

Do I need a loggable wideband?

EJ WRX/STI remote tuning requires an external wideband with an approved way to record AFR or lambda alongside the engine log. The same recording requirement applies to 2015–2017 FA20 WRX remote fueling work; later FA20 and FA24 WRX measurement plans are reviewed individually. A factory oxygen-sensor reading, gauge display or video does not replace the required recorded data. Confirm controller configuration, units and time alignment before testing.

For an approved flex-fuel setup, include ethanol content and other requested channels. Send the kit and current software details, including legacy systems; installed hardware alone does not establish compatibility.

How should I send logs?

Configure and secure the logger while stationary. Collect only the test requested for the current revision. Send original CSV files with headers and units intact, the map/revision name, fuel and a short symptom report. Keep the same inquiry thread. Stop testing if a fault appears; do not repeat aggressive tests through a misfire, unexpected boost, abnormal temperature or fuel smell.

References: COBB Subaru logging overview and COBB monitor definitions. My vehicle-specific instructions take priority over this general preparation list.

BEFORE YOUR SESSION

Pre-dyno and road-tuning checklist

  • Seal the intake: use a regulated, vehicle-appropriate smoke/pressure test. Inspect couplers, intercooler, clamps, bypass valve and vacuum/PCV connections. Repair and retest; never use unrestricted shop air or a guessed pressure.
  • Check ignition: correct plugs and heat range, recent service and gap confirmed for the engine and build. Inspect coils and resolve misfires.
  • Check oil and cooling: correct oil specification and level, current service and no known contamination. Check coolant, fans, hoses and belts; investigate overheating.
  • Verify the battery: healthy battery, charging system, terminals and grounds. It must handle several minutes key-on/engine-off and restart reliably. Follow the flashing tool's stable-power requirements.
  • Confirm fuel: fresh, clean fuel matching the octane and blend communicated to me. Disclose old fuel, additives and recent blend changes.
  • Resolve leaks: no fuel or other hazardous fluid leaks onto hot components, tires or the dyno. Investigate fuel odors before running.
  • Inspect tires and running gear: no exposed cords, bulges, damage or inadequate tread. Check date codes against manufacturer age limits, pressures, wheel fasteners, brakes and steering.
  • Clear the driver area: secure floor mats and cables, remove loose objects and verify unrestricted pedal travel and throttle return.
  • Disclose mechanical issues: warning codes, smoke, fluid consumption, clutch slip and previous damage. Do not clear codes to hide a problem.
  • Agree on the test plan: obey traffic laws for road checks; use a suitable dyno or controlled closed course for full-load testing. Never operate the logger while driving.

If an item fails, pause and contact me. This checklist helps prepare the car; it does not replace a workshop inspection or certify roadworthiness. Download the printable checklist.

Send your setup for review →

You do not need every answer before getting in touch.

Send what you know and identify anything you are unsure about. A useful first conversation starts with an accurate description of your car and what you want to change.

1. Identify the vehicle and ECU

Include year, make, model, engine and transmission. Name the ECU or tuning device, such as a COBB Accessport, Emtron, Haltech or Link ECU. If you are uncertain, a clear photo of its label helps. Platform support depends on the exact vehicle and equipment.

2. List the installed parts

Separate parts already installed from future purchases. Include the turbo, intake, intercooler, fuel system, engine work, sensors and relevant drivetrain changes. Part numbers or shop invoices are useful when names are unclear.

3. Describe fuel and current calibration

State the fuel currently in the tank and what is normally available to you. Include the current map name, previous tuner and any recent hardware changes. Send existing logs if you have them; ask me which information is needed before collecting new ones.

4. Explain the goal or problem

Daily drivability, a specific running issue and a new race build are different starting points. Describe when a symptom happens and share existing fault codes. Include your intended use, timeline and planned upgrades.
